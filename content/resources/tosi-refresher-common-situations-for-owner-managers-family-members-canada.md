## What is TOSI (in plain language)?
TOSI—Tax on Split Income—is a set of Canadian rules that can deny certain income-splitting benefits when family members receive “split income” from a corporation (or other arrangements). In many owner-managed situations, the risk isn’t just about who receives dividends—it’s also about how the business engagement is structured and supported.

This refresher outlines common situations where TOSI questions arise, the types of documentation that help, and practical, general ways to support a reasonable compensation/engagement position.

> Note: This is general guidance. TOSI outcomes can be fact-specific, and you should contact ALOE Accounting and Tax for help reviewing your situation.

## Common TOSI triggers we see in owner-managed businesses
### 1) Paying family members dividends instead of compensation
A common pattern: the corporation earns income, and family members receive dividends rather than salary or wages. If those dividends are viewed as “split income” and none of the reliefs apply, TOSI can come into play.

What to do (practically):
- Understand whether the amounts are dividends, salary, or other forms of consideration.
- Review whether the family member has an ownership or control connection to the corporation.
- Consider whether compensation should reflect actual work performed (see documentation notes below).

### 2) Hiring spouse or adult children for “paper work”
TOSI risk can increase when a family member is paid for limited or unclear services—especially if the role looks like it exists mainly to shift income.

What to do (practically):
- Make sure the role has a real business purpose.
- Ensure the work performed aligns with the pay and hours claimed.

### 3) Paying a family member dividends while they are also involved in the business
If a family member is actively involved (management, operations, decision-making) and receives corporate income, the CRA may focus on whether the income is connected to “split income” concepts.

What to do (practically):
- Document duties, reporting lines, and how the role fits the business.
- Ensure any compensation is supported by evidence of services.

### 4) Using a “contractor” arrangement with family
Some businesses pay family members as contractors (e.g., consulting invoices) instead of through payroll. In many cases, TOSI analysis depends on the substance of the relationship and whether the payment is truly for services.

What to do (practically):
- Have clear engagement agreements and realistic scope.
- Keep evidence of deliverables and who performed the work.

### 5) Salary increases or bonuses that are hard to explain
Even when family members work, TOSI questions can arise if compensation doesn’t look commercially reasonable.

What to do (practically):
- Use a consistent approach to pay based on duties, experience, and time.
- Keep internal support for pay decisions (even if it’s not a formal “benchmark study”).

## Documentation that helps (what to keep and why)
While TOSI isn’t solved by paperwork alone, good documentation makes it easier to support your position.

Consider keeping:
- **Written role descriptions**: responsibilities, title, and scope.
- **Contracts/agreements**: employment agreement, consulting agreement, or engagement letter.
- **Work logs and schedules**: timesheets where appropriate, or documented deliverables.
- **Board minutes and resolutions**: authorizing compensation and approving management decisions.
- **Payroll records** (if applicable): ROEs, T4s, pay stubs, remittances, and documentation of start dates.
- **Invoices and proof of work** (if contractor): deliverables, email correspondence showing work performed, project summaries.
- **Organizational charts/reporting**: who supervises whom and how authority is exercised.

## Practical ways to structure “reasonable” compensation/engagement
Every case is different, but these general steps reduce avoidable risk:

1) **Match pay to actual services**
If the family member is working, align compensation with the work performed (duties, hours, deliverables, and experience).

2) **Avoid “create-a-job” scenarios**
If there’s no genuine role, it’s harder to defend the arrangement. Build roles around business needs, not only tax planning.

3) **Use arm’s-length thinking, even within the family**
You don’t need to copy third-party contracts, but you should be able to explain why the compensation level makes sense.

4) **Separate ownership from employment/contracting**
Ownership and receiving corporate income can’t be ignored in TOSI analysis. If a family member is both an owner and an employee/contractor, be prepared to show the service component is real.

5) **Review regularly—not just at tax time**
Compensation and engagement should make sense throughout the year. Adjust roles and documentation when responsibilities change.

## Next steps for owner-managers
If you’re paying family members, receiving dividends as a family group, or considering hiring relatives, start with a focused review:
- What form of income are family members receiving (salary, dividends, or other)?
- What work is actually being performed, by whom, and when?
- Do you have clear support that compensation is tied to services and is commercially reasonable?
