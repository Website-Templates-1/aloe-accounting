## Why worker status matters
In Canada, whether someone is an employee, an independent contractor, or part of a third-party arrangement affects more than your payroll process. It can impact:

- Payroll remittances (for employees)
- GST/HST invoicing and input tax credits (depending on the setup)
- Income tax reporting
- Potential penalties and interest if CRA believes workers were misclassified

If you are hiring now or using more contractors, it is worth setting up the right documentation and workflow from the start. If you want support, our [accounting and payroll](/services/accounting-payroll) team can help you organize the details.

## Employee vs contractor, the practical factors CRA looks at
CRA does not rely on one “magic” label in a contract. It generally looks at the total relationship. Common factors include:

### 1) Control and supervision
- **Employee:** You control how, when, and where the work is done. You supervise day-to-day.
- **Contractor:** The worker has meaningful control over how the work is performed.

Example: If you set exact hours, require attendance, and provide daily instructions, the relationship may look like employment.

### 2) Ownership of tools and ability to subcontract
- **Employee:** Employer provides tools and cannot usually delegate tasks.
- **Contractor:** Worker typically supplies tools and may hire subcontractors or arrange help (subject to the agreement).

### 3) Integration into your business
- **Employee:** Work is part of your core operations and is integrated into your regular business.
- **Contractor:** Work supports specific projects or deliverables and is less integrated.

### 4) Risk and profit
- **Employee:** Paid a salary or hourly rate, with less financial risk tied to performance.
- **Contractor:** Fees may be based on deliverables, and the worker bears more business risk (for example, costs to complete the job, ability to profit or lose).

### 5) Payment structure and reporting
- **Employee:** Payroll deductions and T4 reporting.
- **Contractor:** Typically invoiced for services, reported via T4A (for amounts that meet reporting thresholds).

Important: Cash flow preference is not a status factor. The substance matters.

## Documentation to support your chosen relationship
Good documentation does not guarantee CRA agreement, but it helps you show the actual working arrangement. Consider keeping:

### Written agreement (before the work starts)
Include details such as:
- Scope of work and deliverables
- Reporting lines (who the worker answers to)
- Work schedule rules (if any) and location requirements
- Payment terms (hourly vs fixed fee, invoicing frequency)
- Ownership of materials and IP (if relevant)
- Confidentiality and non-solicitation clauses (if applicable)
- Termination terms

### Evidence of how the relationship actually operated
Contracts are not enough if your day-to-day practices contradict them. Keep records like:
- Emails or instructions showing level of control
- Time tracking only if it reflects genuine requirements (and not disguised employment)
- Meeting notes that clarify whether the worker is managing their own method
- Proof of invoices and payment records for contractors

### Records checklist for contractor files
For each contractor, maintain:
- Contractor agreement and amendments
- Invoices and proof of payment
- Any certificates or supporting info you require for your GST/HST process
- A log summarizing how the work was delivered (high level is fine)

## When third-party services are different
Sometimes you are not hiring a worker directly. You purchase a service from a third-party provider (for example, a staffing firm, outsourced bookkeeping team, or IT managed services). In these cases:

- You pay the third-party company for services.
- The third-party typically controls staffing, scheduling, and how work is performed.
- You may interact with personnel, but the operational control is usually with the third party.

This can reduce the risk of misclassification, but it is not automatic. CRA can still assess the real relationship if you effectively direct the individuals.

## How CRA reviews misclassification
CRA generally looks beyond labels. If CRA audits and believes workers were employees, they can assess back taxes and penalties, including:

- Payroll source deductions (for the period reviewed)
- Interest and penalties
- Additional reporting corrections

CRA often focuses on whether your practices match your written agreement. Common “red flags” include:
- Control of day-to-day work like an employer
- Mandatory hours or attendance under your direction
- Using contractors to fill ongoing roles with the same structure as employees
- Lack of deliverables, invoicing, or independence

## A simple decision checklist for hiring
Before you onboard anyone, ask:

1) Who controls how, when, and where the work is done?
2) Does the worker provide tools or use their own processes?
3) Are you hiring for a specific deliverable or ongoing integration into your team?
4) How are you paying, and does it reflect business risk?
5) Do your contracts match your actual practice?
6) Are your records complete and consistent?

If you cannot answer these confidently, it is a good time to pause and tighten the structure.

## FAQ

### Is it legal to call someone a contractor in the contract?
It can be, but CRA looks at the real relationship. The contract label alone does not determine status.

### If I pay a contractor hourly, does that automatically mean employee status?
Not automatically. Hourly fees can still fit a contractor relationship if the overall facts show independence and control sits with the worker.

### What if the contractor works on-site at my location?
On-site work is possible for contractors. The key issue is whether you control how and when the work is performed in an employment-like way.

### What records should we keep for contractors?
Keep the signed agreement, invoices, proof of payment, and evidence of how the relationship operated (scope, deliverables, and level of control).

### How does a staffing agency change the analysis?
If the staffing firm controls the workers and schedules and you are purchasing a service, the relationship may be different. CRA can still review what happens in practice.

### What is the biggest cause of CRA issues for misclassification?
Often, it is a mismatch between the written contract and the actual day-to-day instructions and supervision.
