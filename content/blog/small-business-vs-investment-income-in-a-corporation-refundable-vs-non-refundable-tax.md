Running a Canadian corporation as an owner-manager usually means you plan around your **small business income**, the portion of income your corporation earns from an active business. But if your corporation also earns **investment or passive income** (for example, interest, dividends from portfolio investments, or certain rental-type receipts), the tax story can change.

In particular, passive income may lead to different types of corporate tax and, depending on the year’s facts and the corporation’s tax profile, you may see amounts described as **refundable** versus **non-refundable**.

This post explains the concepts at a high level so you can spot issues early and ask the right questions.

## 1) Active business income vs investment income: the starting point

For many Canadian owner-managed businesses, the tax outcome is influenced by whether the corporation earns:

- **Active business income** (generally eligible for the small business deduction and lower corporate tax rates), or
- **Investment/passive income** (often taxed differently, and not always eligible for the same preferences).

A corporation can have both in the same tax year. That mix matters when you look at year-end tax calculations and what the corporation paid during the year.

If you want to understand how corporate taxes are handled more broadly (including timing), see when [corporate tax instalments](/blog/corporate-tax-instalments-when-required-how-to-avoid-penalties) are required.

## 2) Why “refundable” can show up with investment income

When corporate tax is **refundable**, it generally means some portion of the corporate tax paid is intended to be recovered later, often when the corporation pays out funds to shareholders (for example, through dividends) or otherwise meets certain conditions.

When corporate tax is **non-refundable**, it generally means the corporation (or its shareholders) can’t recover that portion in the same way later.

At a practical level, refundable/non-refundable labels are tied to specific tax rules and the way different categories of income are taxed. With investment income, corporate tax may result in refundable components under certain regimes, while other categories can create a more “final” tax cost.

## 3) What this means for owner-managers: cashflow and tax planning

Even if the overall tax outcome depends on the corporation’s detailed facts, there are common planning themes:

- **Year-end planning matters.** Decisions that affect how income is earned and where it sits in the corporation vs personally can influence which tax buckets apply.
- **Pay attention to the corporation’s “tax accounts.”** Refundable and non-refundable outcomes connect to how your corporation’s balances track over time.
- **Consider the role of investment income in your integrated plan.** Many owner-managers think in terms of “corporate vs personal tax.” But the corporation’s internal tax characteristics (including refundable vs non-refundable amounts) can affect what happens later when you extract funds.

For example, if the corporation retains significant investment income rather than paying it out, the pattern of corporate tax paid (and whether any of it may be refundable later) can affect overall outcomes.

If your corporation’s investments are new or growing, it can be worth reviewing your approach with your accountant as part of [tax planning](/services/tax-planning).

## 4) Watch the common triggers that change the tax profile

Investment income inside a corporation can come from many sources. Without getting into advice, these are common triggers that can shift what taxes apply:

- **Large interest income** from cash holdings or GICs
- **Dividends** from portfolio investments
- **Rental or similar income** (depending on the facts, it may not behave like active business income for tax purposes)
- **Timing and allocation** issues (income recognition timing can affect what belongs in the year)

These items may also change how your corporation prepares its financial statements and tax workpapers. If you’re trying to stay audit-ready and reduce surprises, start with clean, [audit-ready bookkeeping records](/blog/cra-bookkeeping-for-owner-managed-businesses-audit-ready-records).

## 5) Why bookkeeping and classification are more important than ever

Refundable vs non-refundable outcomes are sensitive to correct categorization. Misclassification of income, missing supporting documentation, or unclear year-end allocation can create delays and corrections.

A practical workflow for owner-managers often includes:

1. **Confirm how investment income is recorded** in your accounting system
2. **Reconcile statements** (bank, broker/portfolio, and any related accounts)
3. **Ensure income is classified** consistently with how tax will be computed
4. **Review year-end workpapers** before the tax return is filed

## 6) Planning “worth considering” (high level)

At a general level, planning is often most useful when you:

- Expect your corporation’s **investment income to increase** in the coming year
- Have **large retained earnings** and are deciding how to manage cash
- Are considering whether to **change investment holdings** or how income is generated
- Want to improve how you coordinate **business operations, distributions, and tax timing**

Because refundable vs non-refundable tax hinges on specific rules and your corporation’s profile, it’s best to discuss your situation with professionals before making changes.

## Next step: ask the right questions at year-end

When you meet with your tax advisor, consider asking:

- How much of our income was active business vs investment/passive for tax purposes?
- Did we generate any refundable tax components in the year, and under what categories?
- How do the corporate tax accounts affect what happens when we pay dividends later?
- Are our records and classification accurate enough to support the return?
