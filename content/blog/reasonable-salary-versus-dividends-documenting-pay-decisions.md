## Why CRA asks about salary versus dividends
If you own and control a Canadian corporation (T2 filing), CRA may review how much you pay yourself. In particular, CRA can ask whether your compensation includes an appropriate salary for the work you do, with the remaining benefit taken as dividends.

This is not about forcing a single “correct” number. Instead, CRA generally expects you to be able to show that your compensation approach makes sense for your role, your business results, and the corporation’s ability to pay.

For a smooth audit trail, the key is documentation. Before you debate the amount, make sure you can clearly explain the decisions you made and why.

## What “reasonable salary” usually means in practice
In owner-manager situations, CRA typically expects salary to reflect compensation for services you personally provide to the corporation, such as managing operations, sales, administration, or technical work.

Dividends are generally viewed differently. Dividends are paid out of after-tax corporate earnings and reflect investment risk and ownership. They are not meant to be a replacement for paying yourself for employment services.

CRA does not usually start with a specific formula like “X dollars per job title.” Instead, the review often focuses on whether the split between salary and dividends appears reasonable for the circumstances.

## A practical approach to setting pay
Here is a practical workflow you can use each year (or when circumstances change).

### 1) Start with what you do (your role, hours, and responsibilities)
Write down a short “position summary” for your role, including:
- Your main responsibilities
- The time you spend working in the business (even ranges like 30 to 40 hours per week help)
- Any specialized duties tied to your work (pricing, client delivery, compliance, etc.)

This becomes the baseline for why you are paid at all, and why salary is tied to services.

### 2) Consider the corporation’s ability to pay
Salary is an expense of the corporation. Dividends are paid only if the corporation has sufficient after-tax earnings.

When documenting, link your compensation to business reality:
- Current and projected profitability
- Cash flow constraints
- Any seasonality or one-time events that affected earnings

If the year’s results were unusual, note that in your file.

### 3) Use objective support when setting the salary range
Even if you do not match a public salary to a specific job description, you should still document your reasoning. Examples of supports that often help:
- Compensation ranges from credible sources you consulted
- Prior years’ pay (and whether your workload changed)
- Board-approved compensation rationale
- Changes in responsibilities during the year

If your salary changed significantly, include the trigger (new role, new client segment, expanded hours, hiring support, or reduced workload).

### 4) Decide your dividends based on after-tax earnings and cash needs
After-tax earnings and distributable surplus drive the dividend discussion. Document:
- The corporation’s dividend capacity based on your financial statements
- Whether dividends were intended to supplement your personal income planning
- Any shareholder loan considerations (if applicable)

## The documentation CRA expects to see (checklist)
When CRA reviews, it is often trying to confirm the story is consistent, and decisions were not arbitrary. Consider keeping a “Compensation Decision Memo” (one to three pages) with:

### Core items
- Your annual role summary (what services you provided)
- Estimated time commitment or workload ranges
- The corporation’s financial results summary (profitability context)
- Salary rationale (why the salary level is reasonable for your role)
- Dividend rationale (how dividends align with after-tax earnings and surplus)

### Governance items
- Any board minutes approving compensation and dividends
- Payroll records and remittance history for salary
- Dividend declarations and supporting documentation

### Consistency items
- A clear link between changes in pay and changes in responsibilities or business performance
- Notes on unusual events (large one-time costs, major delays, or extraordinary income)

## Common “red flags” to avoid
These are themes that can cause follow-up questions:
- Salary set extremely low compared to the owner-manager workload, with most value taken as dividends
- Salary and dividends that do not track business results or the corporation’s ability to pay
- Missing board approval, missing dividend declarations, or incomplete payroll documentation
- Big unexplained swings year over year without a documented reason

The goal is not perfection. It is consistency, and a file you can produce quickly.

## Bottom line
CRA may review whether salary and dividends match the services you personally provide and the corporation’s financial situation. You cannot always control every outcome, but you can control how well you document the reasoning.

A simple compensation memo, consistent governance records, and objective support for salary and dividends can go a long way toward making your position clear.
