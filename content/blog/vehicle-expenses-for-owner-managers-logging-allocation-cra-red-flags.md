## Why vehicle expenses get reviewed
Vehicle expenses are a common area where CRA asks questions. The goal is not to “make the numbers work”, it is to support the business portion of costs with records. For owner-managers, that means being clear about the difference between personal driving and business driving, and then allocating costs accordingly.

If you want a recordkeeping baseline for audits, see [audit ready records for owner managed businesses](/blog/cra-bookkeeping-for-owner-managed-businesses-audit-ready-records).

## Step 1, split personal versus business use
CRA generally expects you to claim only the portion of expenses that relates to business use.

### What counts as business use
Common examples include:
- Driving to meet clients or customers
- Travel between job sites or customer locations
- Deliveries, service calls, or business errands
- Running business related errands during a workday

### What counts as personal use
Typical personal use includes:
- Commuting between your home and your regular workplace (if applicable)
- Private errands, weekends, and vacation travel
- Any personal trips that are not connected to earning business income

### Key logging tip
You do not need perfect memory, but you do need a reliable system. A log should capture at minimum:
- Date
- Destination (and purpose)
- Kilometres or starting and ending odometer readings
- Whether the trip was business or personal

## Step 2, choose how you calculate the business portion
There are two common approaches used by owner-managers:

1. **Actual expenses method**: You total eligible vehicle costs (for example fuel, maintenance, insurance, lease costs, repairs, parking, and tolls), then apply the business use percentage.
2. **Kilometre based method**: You use a log to support business kilometres, then apply the business kilometres to determine the deductible portion (subject to the rules that apply to your situation).

The right method depends on your facts and how the vehicle is used. CRA’s position will focus on whether the calculation is reasonable and supported by your log.

## Step 3, understand how the claim shows up for tax purposes
Vehicle treatment can differ depending on whether you operate through a corporation or as a sole proprietor.

### Sole proprietor or partnership
- Vehicle expenses are typically claimed as business expenses on your personal return.
- The deduction generally reflects the business portion you can support.

### Incorporated owner-manager (corporation)
A corporation may deduct eligible vehicle expenses, again limited to the business-use portion. There are also situations where the corporation pays the owner for automobile use under specific arrangements.

If you are unsure how your vehicle claim should be structured when you are incorporated, discuss it with your tax advisor. Our [corporate tax](/services/corporate-tax) team can walk through common structures and documentation expectations.

## Step 4, recordkeeping expectations CRA usually looks for
When CRA reviews vehicle claims, they often focus on three things: support, consistency, and reasonableness.

Practical things to keep:
- A contemporaneous log of business versus personal kilometres
- Fuel receipts and maintenance invoices, when you can get them
- Lease or loan statements (if applicable) and insurance records
- Parking and toll receipts
- Notes showing the business purpose of trips, not just destinations

For housekeeping, also make sure your bookkeeping matches your tax position. If your general ledger coded “everything vehicle related” but your tax return claimed only business use, keep the workpapers or reconciliation.

For a broader approach to getting audit-ready, use [audit ready records for owner managed businesses](/blog/cra-bookkeeping-for-owner-managed-businesses-audit-ready-records).

## Common CRA red flags to avoid
Here are frequent problem areas that can trigger questions or adjustments:

1. **No log, or a log created after the fact**
If you cannot show how the business percentage was determined, claims are harder to defend.

2. **Overstated business use**
For example, reporting most driving as business without explaining the pattern and purpose.

3. **Missing receipts or incomplete documentation**
Not every item has to be retained forever, but you should be able to reconstruct key amounts and support major costs.

4. **Confusing commuting with business use**
Commuting is a frequent misunderstanding.

5. **Claiming non-eligible costs**
Some costs may not be deductible in the way you expect. If you are including expenses that feel “vehicle related” but are personal or capital in nature, get clarity.

6. **Inconsistent treatment across years**
Sudden large changes to business kilometres or deduction method without explanation can draw attention.

## GST/HST and vehicle expenses (brief reminder)
If you are registered for GST/HST, eligibility of input tax credits (ITCs) depends on whether expenses are related to taxable supplies and other rules. Vehicle use can affect the ITC position, especially where there is mixed-use.

If this is part of your situation, talk to the firm so you can align your bookkeeping with the tax position. Our [CRA representation](/services/cra-representation) and advisory services can also be helpful if you have already received questions.

## How to reduce risk without overcomplicating
- Start with a simple trip log you will actually complete.
- Use the same method year over year unless your facts change.
- Keep receipts for major costs and track minor items consistently.
- Reconcile your log to your bookkeeping, so your tax return is traceable.

## Quick checklist
- Log business trips with date, destination, purpose, and odometer info
- Calculate a reasonable business-use percentage
- Claim only the business portion of eligible vehicle costs
- Keep invoices and records, and reconcile to your general ledger
- Review for personal-use creep, commuting confusion, and missing support
