## Why home office claims get denied
Home office expenses are one of the most common areas where CRA requests receipts, asks for calculations, or disallows the claim. The usual reasons are:

- The work-from-home arrangement doesn’t meet CRA’s rules (or it’s not clearly documented)
- Expenses aren’t supported with reasonable records
- The calculation method is wrong or applied inconsistently
- People include costs that are personal, capital, or not eligible

The key is that **employees and incorporated owner-managers (corporations) are treated differently**.

## Employees working from home (T777 worksheet)
If you’re an employee, your home office claim generally flows through your personal return. CRA allows a deduction for eligible expenses using **either a simplified method or a detailed method** (you choose the approach that fits your facts).

### Eligible expenses (typical categories)
In general, employees may claim the business-use portion of eligible costs such as:

- **Rent** (if you pay rent)
- **Utilities** such as electricity and heating, and often internet (CRA may require you to show business use)
- **Home insurance**
- **Property taxes** (where applicable)
- **Maintenance and minor repairs** related to the home office area
- **Office supplies** (directly related to earning employment income)
- **A reasonable portion of telephone/internet**, where there is a business-use element

What you usually cannot claim (or must be careful with):
- **Personal expenses** with no business-use allocation
- Expenses that are **capital** in nature or relate to increasing the value of the home (these rules differ from current operating costs)
- Amounts not tied to earning employment income

### Two CRA approaches: simplified vs detailed
CRA generally allows two ways to calculate your deduction:

- **Simplified method** (often easier):
  - You use a flat calculation based on the **number of eligible hours** you worked from home and a **rate set by CRA rules**.
  - You still need to qualify for the simplified eligibility and keep basic support.

- **Detailed method**:
  - You calculate the **actual eligible expenses** and apply the **business-use percentage**.
  - You determine your home office portion (commonly based on **square footage** of the workspace relative to the home).

### Common employee pitfalls
- **Claiming too much area**: using a “whole room” or “whole basement” allocation even if only a portion is used for work.
- **No clear business-use basis**: e.g., no explanation for how the workspace is used to earn your employment income.
- **Mixing personal and business**: forgetting to apply the allocation to utilities and insurance.
- **Missing support**: CRA may ask for receipts or for how you calculated the business-use percentage.

## Incorporated owner-managers (corporation pays, then your personal return may still be relevant)
If you operate through a corporation, the corporation may be able to deduct certain home office costs **if it has the right to claim expenses related to earning its income**. Whether you claim personally (and how) depends on your arrangement.

### Typical structures
Most owner-managers fall into one of these patterns:

1. **Corporation reimburses you** (accountable plan style)
   - You submit expenses and the corporation deducts the reimbursed amount.
   - You must be able to show the business-use portion and maintain records.

2. **You pay personally, then you recover amounts**
   - The corporation may reimburse based on documentation.
   - This can look similar to (1), but the timing and documentation matter.

3. **You claim directly personally**
   - This is more sensitive for incorporated owner-managers. Even if you work from home, personal claims are not always the right mechanism for corporate owners, and CRA expectations can be different.

Because rules depend on your exact facts (and the way your corporation and employment/fees are set up), it’s best to have a clear written approach.

### Eligible costs for owner-managers (what’s usually in scope)
Where home office costs are deductible or reimbursable, they generally follow the same logic as employee claims: only **the business-use portion** of eligible expenses.

Common categories include:

- Rent (if you rent your home)
- Utilities (hydro/gas/water where connected to the work area)
- Home internet and sometimes phone (depending on usage)
- Home insurance
- Property taxes
- Minor maintenance and repairs
- Office supplies

Be careful with:
- **Capital improvements**: these may not be treated the same way as monthly operating expenses.
- **Laundry/cleaning** and other costs: only include if you can justify a business-use link (and the allocation is reasonable).

### Common owner-manager pitfalls
- **Using personal reimbursement practices that don’t match corporate deductibility**
- **No business-use support**: CRA expects a reasonable methodology for allocating expenses.
- **Claiming corporate deductions without documentation**: receipts, calculation worksheets, and a consistent business-use basis are critical.
- **Inconsistent calculations year over year**: if your allocation method changes without explanation, CRA may ask questions.

## Practical checklist before you claim
Whether you’re an employee or an incorporated owner-manager, you’ll want to:

- Keep **receipts/statements** for key expenses (utilities, insurance, rent, internet)
- Document your **work pattern** (especially for a simplified hours-based method)
- Use a **reasonable allocation** for the business-use portion (often square footage)
- Separate **capital vs operating** items and don’t assume all improvements are deductible
- Avoid claiming expenses for areas used solely for personal activities
