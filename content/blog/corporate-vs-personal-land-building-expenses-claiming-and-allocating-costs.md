## Why land and building expenses get complicated
Owner-managers often pay property related costs personally at first, then later have the corporation use the space. Or they have the corporation own the property and wonder which costs can be claimed and how to document them.

In Canada, the key tax issue is whether the owner of the property is you personally, your corporation, or a combination, and whether the expense is a deductible expense, a capital expense, or an expense that must be allocated.

If you are unsure about your situation, ask your accountant to confirm the correct treatment for your facts and your fiscal year-end.

For broader tax-year record readiness, see [audit ready records](/blog/cra-bookkeeping-for-owner-managed-businesses-audit-ready-records).

## Step 1: Identify who owns the property
### Property owned personally
If you personally own the land and building, then, generally, the tax reporting flows through you personally. That usually means:
- You pay expenses personally (or reimburse yourself).
- Any deductions, if available, are claimed on your personal tax return, not your corporation’s return.
- If the corporation uses your personally owned property, there may be rent paid to you by the corporation.

A common pitfall is the corporation trying to deduct costs that belong to your personal ownership without an appropriate arrangement (such as rent). This can create deductions that do not match how the transaction is actually structured.

### Property owned through a corporation
If your corporation owns the property, the corporation generally claims the property related expenses on its corporate tax return, subject to the tax rules for deductible expenses and capital cost allowance (CCA).

However, not everything is automatically deductible. Land is not depreciable for Canadian tax purposes, and certain costs are treated as capital improvements rather than current expenses.

For help coordinating filings, consider [corporate tax support](/services/corporate-tax).

## Step 2: Separate deductible expenses from capital items
Many property costs fall into one of three categories. The mix matters for both personal and corporate owners.

### 1) Current deductible expenses (where applicable)
Examples often include:
- Routine repairs and maintenance to keep the property in good working order
- Property taxes, where they meet the general requirements
- Insurance
- Utilities, if the corporation is responsible for them

### 2) Capital improvements (generally not deductible immediately)
Capital improvements typically create or enhance an asset. Examples might include:
- Major renovations
- Structural changes
- Additions that extend the building’s useful life

These are often capital in nature and may be added to the asset’s tax basis and potentially claimed over time through CCA.

A common pitfall is calling an improvement a repair. CRA can challenge this when the work increases the value, materially extends the life, or upgrades the asset.

### 3) Items that require careful allocation
Some costs relate to both business and non-business use, or to both land and building.

A frequent allocation issue is when only part of the property is used for the business (or when the property is partially rented to others). You will typically need a reasonable allocation method based on square footage, time, or another supportable basis.

If you also claim space-related deductions for home or a separate work area, you may find this helpful: [home office expense rules](/blog/claiming-home-office-expenses-canada-employees-vs-incorporated-owner-managers).

## Step 3: Handle rent and reimbursements correctly
### When you own personally, and the corporation uses the space
Often the cleanest approach is:
- The corporation pays you rent (if the corporation is using your personally owned property)
- You pay the property costs personally
- You can reimburse yourself via rent income and appropriate personal reporting

Pitfalls include:
- The corporation paying expenses directly but not paying rent.
- The corporation treating personal property costs as if they are corporation-owned expenses without an underlying rental arrangement.
- Inadequate documentation (no lease or missing terms).

If the corporation reimburses you for expenses, it should be supported and consistent with how the arrangement is described in your books. This is one area where unclear paperwork can lead to questions.

For support on documentation and compliance, review [CRA representation](/services/cra-representation).

### When the corporation owns the property
Where the corporation owns the building, it should generally:
- Record property expenses in the corporate books
- Track ownership and responsibility in a consistent way
- Separate capital improvements from deductible repairs

Pitfalls include:
- Paying a shareholder personally for costs that should be corporate expenses (or vice versa)
- Mixing personal and corporate use in a way that is not properly allocated

## Step 4: Watch the land portion
Land is not depreciable. If you purchase property (or if you later make changes to the property), make sure you can support what portion relates to land versus the building.

A practical takeaway: keep closing documents, appraisals, and any allocation schedules where available. When the corporation owns the property, this matters for how CCA is computed.

## Step 5: Keep records the CRA can follow
Regardless of whether you own personally or through a corporation, you should be able to show:
- Who owns the property (deed, corporate minute records if applicable)
- A clear business use story (lease terms if rent is paid)
- A summary of monthly property costs
- Support for the nature of work (repair versus improvement)
- A reasonable allocation method when costs are mixed

## Bottom line
- Ownership drives the tax reporting, not just who pays the invoice.
- Repairs and maintenance are often deductible, improvements are usually capital.
- Rent and reimbursements must match the way the arrangement is structured and documented.
- Allocation is essential when costs relate to both business and non-business use, or both land and building.
