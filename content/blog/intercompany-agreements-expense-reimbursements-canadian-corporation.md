## When do you need an intercompany agreement?

Inside a Canadian corporate group, you might have costs paid by one corporation and later reimbursed by another. That can work smoothly, but it is important to define the terms upfront.

A formal intercompany agreement is especially important when:

- Two or more corporations are involved, and money will move between them.
- The reimbursement is recurring (for example, shared staff time, rent, software licences, or management fees).
- Costs are allocated based on a method that needs consistency (for example, headcount, usage, or direct project tracking).
- There is any possibility of disagreement about timing, amounts, or documentation.
- The arrangement involves long payment cycles, large balances, or uncertain billing.

Even when the CRA is not automatically looking for written contracts in every situation, written agreements help show that transactions are genuine and commercially reasonable. This is particularly relevant for related parties.

## Expense reimbursements: “who should charge what”

A common setup is this: Corporation A pays expenses, then Corporation B reimburses Corporation A. To support this, each expense should be tracked so you can answer three questions:

1. **What was the expense?** (invoice, receipt, or internal expense log)
2. **Who benefited?** (the corporation or business activity that used the asset or service)
3. **How was the amount determined?** (exact cost, allocation, or documented calculation)

### Direct expenses
If an expense clearly relates to Corporation B (for example, a vendor invoice for Corporation B’s specific contract or a direct project charge), it is often simpler to bill Corporation B directly, or ensure Corporation B reimburses only the portion that relates to it.

### Allocated shared expenses
Some costs are shared, such as:

- Office expenses
- Payroll for shared administrative staff
- Software and subscriptions
- Insurance
- Professional fees (for example, accounting or legal)

For these, the reimbursement should be based on a reasonable allocation method and applied consistently. Examples of allocation bases include:

- Time tracking (where staff hours can be supported)
- Headcount or square footage (for facility-type costs)
- Usage metrics (for IT services)

Keep in mind that allocations are a documentation exercise. If you cannot show your method, the reimbursement is harder to defend.

### Interest and other charges
If corporations lend money to one another or if reimbursements are not settled promptly, you may also be dealing with shareholder loan style issues in the broader structure. ## How to set up the accounting entries

In many owner-managed groups, reimbursements are recorded as:

- Corporation A: expense initially recorded as its own cost, then later recognized as a reimbursement receivable.
- Corporation B: reimbursement recorded as an expense (or reduction of a related cost), and a payable to Corporation A.

To keep things clean:

- Reconcile reimbursement totals monthly or per billing cycle.
- Use consistent descriptions on intercompany invoices or journal entries.
- Avoid netting unrelated balances, unless your accounting policies explicitly allow it.

## GST/HST considerations (where applicable)

GST/HST can be part of intercompany reimbursements depending on the nature of what is being billed and whether input tax credits are available.

General guidance:

- If one entity is reimbursed for a taxable supply it received, the GST/HST treatment may follow the invoicing and underlying tax rules.
- If you are sharing costs internally, you still need to consider whether you are effectively making a taxable supply.

## What documentation should you keep?

To support reimbursements in the event of a question from CRA (or a request during year-end review), keep a complete file showing:

- **Intercompany agreement** (signed, dated, describing terms)
- **Expense source documents** (vendor invoices, receipts, contracts)
- **Allocation method** (how the calculation was done, with the supporting worksheet)
- **Intercompany billing or reimbursement invoices**
  - amount and period covered
  - description of services or expense categories
  - due dates and settlement terms
- **Payment evidence** (bank transfers, payment confirmations)
- **General ledger support** (journal entries and account mapping)

A good rule of thumb: if someone else could reconstruct the reimbursement from your records in 30 minutes, you are in good shape.

## Timing and consistency

Reimbursements should be billed and settled according to the agreement. Practical steps include:

- Establish a billing schedule (for example, monthly or quarterly).
- Charge the reimbursement in the same accounting period as the underlying expense when possible.
- Keep the allocation method consistent period to period.

If your corporation regularly has other compliance deadlines, pairing your intercompany process with your annual workflow can reduce year-end surprises. ## Bottom line

Intercompany expense reimbursements inside a Canadian corporate group are manageable when you have clear terms and a defensible method. Formal agreements are most important for recurring, allocated, or high-dollar arrangements. Then, support every reimbursement with invoices, a documented allocation, and consistent intercompany billing and settlement.
